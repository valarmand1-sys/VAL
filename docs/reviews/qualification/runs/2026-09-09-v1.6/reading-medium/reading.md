## O5

**Prompt:** In about two hundred words, what does a good table read need from the room — practical things, not sentiment.

**Criterion:** 150–250 words; ≥3 concrete practical items; no sentiment padding

### Answer

My lord, the practical requirements are few and mostly logistical.

Everyone works from the same draft, with the same pagination and the same date on the title page. Nothing wastes a read faster than three versions in the room.

Cast every part, including the small ones, and give stage directions to someone plain-spoken who won't perform them. If you can avoid reading a lead yourself, do — you need your attention on the room, not the page.

Run it once, straight through, without stopping. No preamble explaining what a scene is meant to do, and no notes given mid-read. Time it, and mark the clock in the margin at act breaks so you know where the length actually sits.

Someone other than you takes notes, because you'll be listening. What you're marking is behaviour, not opinion: where laughs land and where they don't, where people shift or check out, where a reader stumbles on a line twice — that's usually the line, not the reader.

Keep the room small and pick people who will tell you the truth. A dozen polite friends will teach you nothing.

Water on the table, phones away, and discussion afterwards — restricted, in my view, to what was heard rather than what was intended.

## O9

**Prompt:** We have nine shooting days, a lead available for six of them, and two locations three hours apart — the harbour and the clockmaker's workshop. Sketch a schedule shape and say what breaks first if the weather turns.

**Criterion:** a specific nine-day shape accounting for both locations and the company move; the lead's six days placed; the first weather-driven failure named with a reason (a move of any length passes if coherently accounted for) — MANDATORY

### Answer

My lord, two things I don't know and am assuming: that the harbour is exterior and weather-exposed while the workshop is a controllable interior, and that the lead's six days are ours to choose rather than fixed dates. If either is wrong, the shape changes materially.

**The shape**

| Days | Unit | Lead |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | Harbour | Yes |
| 5 | Company move | — |
| 6–7 | Workshop | Yes |
| 8–9 | Workshop | No |

One company move. The lead's six days sit contiguous across the move, which spares you travel-day-plus-shoot-day for her. The three days without her go at the tail, in the interior, where they are the least weather-sensitive material in the picture: mechanisms, hands, clock faces, inserts, other cast, anything that plays without her.

The move day is a genuine loss of a ninth of the schedule and I'd try to buy it back — pre-rig the workshop with a small crew while the harbour block runs, so day 6 is a shooting day and not a lighting day.

**What breaks first**

Not the schedule. The lead's sixth day.

Every other constraint here is soft. Weather days can be reordered, the workshop can absorb overtime, inserts can be shot by anyone. The one thing that cannot flex is that she walks after her sixth day, and the three days remaining to you afterward are precisely the days she cannot help you recover. So a lost harbour day doesn't cost you a day at the harbour — it compresses into days 6 and 7 at the workshop, which are already carrying the workshop's lead coverage. Lose two harbour days and you are cutting scenes or cutting her out of them.

Second to break is continuity. The moment you start moving harbour scenes between a grey day and a bright one to chase the weather, the sequence stops cutting together, and that damage is invisible until the edit.

**What I'd do about it**

- Buy an option on a seventh day from her now, while it's cheap. It is the single highest-value thing on this list and it costs nothing unless you use it.
- Find a covered set within thirty minutes of the harbour — a chandlery, a harbourmaster's office, a wheelhouse. Three hours apart means the workshop is not cover for anything. Without local cover, a rained-off day is a lost day, not a moved one.
- At the harbour, shoot her coverage before the wides. Faces need her; establishers, boats, water, and empty-dock plates do not, and can go to days 8 and 9 if the sky cooperates.
- Sort the harbour scenes into rain-viable and dry-required, and spend every clear hour on the dry-required ones. Rain on a harbour is often a gift; it is only a problem when the script says otherwise.

Tell me how much of the harbour material actually needs her in frame, and I can tell you whether four days is right or whether this wants five and a tighter workshop.
