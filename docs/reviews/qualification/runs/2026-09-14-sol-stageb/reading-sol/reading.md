## O5

**Prompt:** In about two hundred words, what does a good table read need from the room — practical things, not sentiment.

**Criterion:** 150–250 words; ≥3 concrete practical items; no sentiment padding

### Answer

A good table read needs a quiet, private room with enough space for everyone to sit comfortably and see one another. Use a large table or an open horseshoe arrangement; avoid classroom rows. Provide printed, paginated scripts with character names clearly marked, plus pencils, highlighters, water, and spare copies.

Assign seats before people arrive. Put actors near scene partners where practical, but keep the reader handling stage directions somewhere central. The director, writer, and note-takers should have clear sightlines without dominating the table.

Control the environment: comfortable temperature, good lighting, disabled notifications, closed doors, and no unnecessary foot traffic. Test any recording equipment beforehand and obtain consent to record. If anyone is joining remotely, use a proper conference microphone and speaker rather than balancing a laptop at one end of the room.

Display a cast list and running order. Name who will read stage directions and cover absent roles. Set scheduled breaks, particularly for anything longer than ninety minutes. Have a clock visible to the facilitator, not looming over the actors.

Finally, appoint one person to run the room. They should begin on time, explain whether interruptions are allowed, keep discussion out of the reading itself, and reserve notes for afterwards. Without that discipline, a table read becomes a workshop by accident, my lord.

## O9

**Prompt:** We have nine shooting days, a lead available for six of them, and two locations three hours apart — the harbour and the clockmaker's workshop. Sketch a schedule shape and say what breaks first if the weather turns.

**Criterion:** a specific nine-day shape accounting for both locations and the company move; the lead's six days placed; the first weather-driven failure named with a reason (a move of any length passes if coherently accounted for) — MANDATORY

### Answer

My lord, I would block the locations rather than attempt a three-hour company move.

### Schedule shape

| Day | Location | Lead | Purpose |
|---|---|---:|---|
| 1 | Harbour | No | Establishing shots, plates, inserts, supporting cast; finalise weather plan |
| 2–5 | Harbour | Yes | All lead exterior work, prioritised by weather and continuity |
| 6 | Workshop | No | Company move, pre-light, inserts and supporting-cast material |
| 7–8 | Workshop | Yes | Lead interior scenes |
| 9 | Workshop | No | Pick-ups, details, wild tracks and contingency |

This spends four of the lead’s six days at the harbour because that is the exposed location; the workshop receives two tightly prepared lead days. Harbour scenes should be ranked so that one of Days 2–5 can be lost without losing the essential material.

### What breaks first

The first thing to break is **harbour continuity involving the lead**. Bad weather can cost a full day, alter sea state and light between matching scenes, and you cannot simply recover that work during the workshop block three hours away. After that, the pressure falls on the two workshop lead days: they become the only place left to compress the schedule.

The dangerous version is one in which the harbour genuinely requires all four lead days. Then there is no weather cover at all—only four scheduled exterior days presented as contingency. I would either design the harbour work to fit three days, or secure a seventh lead hold.
